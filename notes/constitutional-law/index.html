What these notes cover

Complete NCA Constitutional Law coverage — every framework you need for the exam:

  • Charter analysis: s.32 threshold, identification of the right claimed (ss.2, 7-15), s.1 Oakes test (pressing and substantial objective, proportionality — rational connection, minimal impairment, proportionality of effects).
  • Division of powers: Characterisation, pith and substance, POGG (gap, national concern, emergency), trade and commerce, criminal law power.
  • Cooperative and competitive federalism: Paramountcy, interjurisdictional immunity, referral procedure, severance.
  • Aboriginal rights (s.35): Van der Peet test, Haida consultation and accommodation, Sparrow justification framework.
  • Section 33 notwithstanding clause: When it applies, its temporal limit.
  • Full answer template: Structured framework for Charter, division of powers, and Aboriginal rights questions.

The Charter analysis structure

Most NCA Constitutional Law exam questions require a Charter analysis. The answer structure is always:

  1. Is the s.32 threshold met? (Government actor or legislated conduct?)
  2. Identify the right or freedom at issue (s.2, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 15)
  3. Is the right infringed? (Apply the right-specific test)
  4. Is the infringement justified under s.1? (Oakes test)
  5. If not saved by s.1, consider s.33 notwithstanding clause.
  6. What is the remedy under s.24?

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Constitutional Law the hardest NCA subject?

Constitutional Law is consistently rated among the most challenging NCA subjects. The Oakes test, division of powers analysis, and Aboriginal rights framework are substantial bodies of doctrine. Most candidates require 100-140 hours of dedicated study.

How is the Canadian Charter different from other constitutions?

The Canadian Charter (1982) is entrenched and directly enforceable by courts. It contains a unique s.1 justification mechanism (the Oakes test) and a legislative override clause (s.33). The s.24 remedies provision allows courts to exclude evidence.

Does Constitutional Law overlap with Administrative Law?

Yes. The Dore framework (2012 SCC) applies when an administrative decision-maker exercises statutory discretion that engages Charter values — it bridges Constitutional and Administrative Law.
