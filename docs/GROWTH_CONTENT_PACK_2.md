## 1. SECOND GUEST POST — the credibility/story angle

Pitch this to a *different* blog than guest post #1 (which was the schedule/how-to angle). This one leads with your story, which is your unfair advantage.

> ### How I Passed Four NCA Exams in Under Three Months (What I'd Tell My Past Self)
>
> When I moved my legal career to Canada, the National Committee on Accreditation assigned me five challenge exams. Everything I read online made it sound like a two-year ordeal. It wasn't. I passed four of the five in under three months, and prepared for my first — Administrative Law — in seven days. Here's what actually made the difference, stripped of the myths.
>
> **1. The NCA is not a knowledge test. It's a technique test.** The exams are open-book and three hours long. The examiner is not checking whether you memorised Canadian law; they're checking whether you can spot the issue, apply the right framework to unfamiliar facts, and write a structured answer under time pressure. Once I understood that, I stopped reading textbooks cover to cover and started building tools I could use *during* the exam.
>
> **2. Tight, tabbed notes beat comprehensive ones.** A 400-page summary is useless when you have 45 minutes per question — you'll spend the exam flipping pages instead of writing. I built summaries under 80 pages per subject, tabbed so I could find any framework in seconds. In an open-book exam, navigation speed is everything.
>
> **3. Learn the two or three frameworks that carry each subject.** In Administrative Law, that's the *Vavilov* standard of review and procedural fairness under *Baker*. In Constitutional Law, it's the division of powers and the *Oakes* test. Master those cold and you've covered the bulk of the exam; everything else is secondary.
>
> **4. Pre-write your answer structure.** For each topic I had a template — issue, rule, application, conclusion — that I could adapt to any fact pattern in seconds. Walking into the exam with the structure already in my head saved 10–15 minutes per question.
>
> **5. Practise under the clock.** Reading feels like studying; it isn't. The first time you write a full *Vavilov* analysis in 45 minutes should not be on exam day. Timed practice is the single most underrated part of NCA prep.
>
> **6. Sequence your subjects deliberately.** Not every subject is offered every month, and results take 10–12 weeks, so the order you sit them in affects your whole timeline. Check the official schedule and plan backwards from when you want to finish.
>
> The NCA is hard, but it is passable and far more predictable than the internet suggests. Respect it, prepare with the right tools, and it opens.
>

### Administrative Law
> **The one distinction that trips up most people on NCA Admin Law**
> After *Vavilov*, the default standard of review is reasonableness — but candidates keep applying it to *procedural* fairness questions, where it doesn't belong. Standard of review (Vavilov) and procedural fairness (Baker) are two separate analyses. If the question is "was the process fair?" you're in Baker-factor territory, not Vavilov. Keep them apart and you'll pick up marks a lot of people leave on the table. Happy to explain the five Vavilov correctness exceptions if useful — I've got a plain-English breakdown here: thencahub.com/blog/vavilov-explained

### Constitutional Law
> **Division of powers: the trap in the "gap branch" of POGG**
> A common misconception: that any *new* subject matter (things that didn't exist in 1867) automatically falls to the federal gap branch. It doesn't — aeronautics, for example, is assigned to Parliament under *national concern*, not the gap branch. Getting POGG's three branches straight (gap, national concern, emergency) is worth real marks. Free walkthrough of the whole division-of-powers analysis (with the General Motors test) here if it helps: thencahub.com/blog/division-of-powers-nca

### Criminal Law
> **NCA Criminal Law: know the *current* intoxication law**
> A lot of older prep material is out of date here. Since *R v Brown* (2022) and Parliament's response, the framework for extreme intoxication and the new s.33.1 is different from what many summaries still say. If your notes predate that, double-check them. I keep an updated breakdown of the defences (self-defence s.34, duress, necessity, intoxication) here: thencahub.com/blog/article-b2-criminal-law-nca

### Professional Responsibility
> **PR is the subject people underestimate most**
> It's not substantive law — it's the Federation's Model Code applied to scenarios. Conflicts of interest, confidentiality (and its four exceptions), and withdrawal come up constantly. The trick is knowing the *rule numbers* and applying them to facts, not writing essays. Free overview of the high-frequency topics here: thencahub.com/blog/article-b3-professional-responsibility-nca

### Foundations of Canadian Law
> **Foundations: the Quebec question people get wrong**
> On the bijural part, don't write that Quebec law is "based on the French Code civil." Quebec's private law is governed by its own **Civil Code of Québec** (in force 1994). That precision costs marks if you miss it. Also nail the modern principle of statutory interpretation (Driedger, affirmed in *Rizzo Shoes*). Free guide: thencahub.com/blog/article-b4-foundations-canadian-law-nca
