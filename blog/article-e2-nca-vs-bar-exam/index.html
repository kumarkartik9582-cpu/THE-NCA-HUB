NCA vs Bar Exam: Key Differences in Format, Content & Strategy (2026)

By Kartik Kumar ·12 min read · Updated: March 2026

NCA exams test Canadian legal substance — bar exams test provincial procedure. Two completely different formats, different strategies, and a mandatory sequence you cannot reverse. Here is everything an internationally trained lawyer needs to understand before starting either.

The short answer: NCA exams and provincial bar exams are entirely separate gates to Canadian legal practice. The NCA comes first — always. It tests whether your substantive knowledge of Canadian law is equivalent to a Canadian law graduate. The bar exam comes after, and tests whether you know the procedural rules of the specific province where you want to practise. The formats, the preparation strategies, and the consequences of confusing the two are completely different. You must pass both, in order.

What the NCA Exam Actually Tests

The National Committee on Accreditation (NCA) is a standing committee of the Federation of Law Societies of Canada. Its job is to determine whether an internationally trained lawyer's legal education is equivalent to a Canadian law school graduate's. The NCA exams are how that equivalence is demonstrated.

What they test: Substantive Canadian law — the body of legal rules, frameworks, and principles that a Canadian law graduate would know. Not procedure. Not forms. Not filing deadlines. The law itself.

Format: Open-book, long-form written answers (problem questions), 3 hours per subject, sat online through the MonitorEDU proctoring system. You may bring printed hard-copy notes — electronic notes cannot be accessed because the Paradigm Testing secure browser locks your computer down entirely during the exam.

Pass mark: 50%, graded on a pass/fail basis. Results are released approximately 10 to 12 weeks after each exam session. You may write each subject up to three times (one attempt plus two re-writes). A fourth attempt requires a written application under NCA Policies.

Who sets them: The NCA — a standing committee of the Federation of Law Societies of Canada, not any individual provincial law society.

Language: NCA exams may be written in English or French. The Federation of Law Societies is a bilingual organisation. To request a French-language exam, submit a written request to exam@flsc.ca before the registration deadline for that session.

The Five Mandatory Subjects — and What "Mandatory" Actually Means

The NCA assigns all internationally trained lawyers these five core subjects, regardless of where they qualified:

  1. Canadian Administrative Law — the Vavilov (2019) standard of review framework, judicial review, tribunal jurisdiction
  2. Canadian Constitutional Law — Charter rights analysis (Oakes test), division of powers (ss. 91/92), Aboriginal constitutional rights (s. 35)
  3. Canadian Criminal Law — the federal Criminal Code, Charter application in criminal proceedings, sentencing under ss. 718–718.2
  4. Canadian Professional Responsibility — the CBA Model Code of Professional Conduct, LSO Rules of Professional Conduct
  5. Foundations of Canadian Law — Indigenous law, the bijural tradition (common law + civil law), Canadian legal theory and statutory interpretation

These five are the floor, not the ceiling. Based on your individual transcript and jurisdiction, the NCA may assign additional subjects on top of these five. Common additions include Contracts, Torts, Property, Business Organisations, Family Law, or Evidence. Lawyers from mixed-jurisdiction countries — such as the Philippines, where civil law and common law co-exist — typically receive all eight core subjects. Your exact assignment is determined individually by the NCA after reviewing your academic transcripts and professional history.

If your legal qualifications are considered "stale" — meaning you have not been actively engaged in legal work or study for extended periods — additional subjects may also be assigned. The NCA requires that in three of the last five years you were attending a qualifying law programme, engaged in legal work, or both. Verify your specific assignment with the NCA directly at nca.legal/process/assigned-requirements/.

Additionally, since January 1, 2022, all NCA candidates must complete the Legal Research and Writing (LRW) module through CPLED or an approved Canadian law school. And from March 1, 2026, all candidates must also demonstrate competency in Indigenous Law and Peoples (NCA Policy subsection 10.5) before a Certificate of Qualification will be issued.

What the Provincial Bar Exam Actually Tests

The provincial bar exams are set by individual provincial law societies — the Law Society of Ontario (LSO), the Law Society of British Columbia (LSBC), the Law Society of Alberta (LSA), and so on. They are entirely separate from the NCA and operate on completely different logic.

What they test: Procedural competence — whether you know the specific rules of practice in the province where you want to be called to the bar. Filing deadlines. Service rules. Which forms apply. What happens at each stage of civil or criminal litigation in that province.

Format (Ontario example): Open-book, multiple-choice. Two exams: Barrister (civil procedure, criminal procedure, family law procedure) and Solicitor (real estate, wills and estates, business law transactions). Other provinces use different formats — Alberta uses a bar admission course with exams; BC uses the PLTC (Professional Legal Training Course).

Pass mark: Set by each individual provincial law society. The LSO does not publish a specific percentage cut score — it is not appropriate to state a universal figure. Verify the current pass threshold directly with the law society of your target province before you begin studying.

Who sets them: Individual provincial law societies — not the NCA, not the Federation of Law Societies.

Preparation Strategy: Why They Are Completely Different

NCA Preparation Strategy

The NCA is an application exam. You are given a fact pattern and asked to apply the law to it. A candidate who memorises hundreds of cases but cannot structure a coherent legal argument will fail. A candidate who knows the analytical frameworks fluently — and can apply them to an unfamiliar set of facts in 45–60 minutes per question — will pass.

The core preparation skills for NCA exams:

  • Framework mastery: You must be able to write the Vavilov standard of review analysis, the Oakes test, the Baker procedural fairness factors, and other core frameworks from memory, under timed conditions, without hesitation.
  • Application practice: Timed writing of full problem question answers is the single most important preparation activity. Reading and highlighting notes is the least effective. See our guide on how many hours to study for the NCA exam.
  • Retrieval speed: Because notes are permitted but the clock is running, what matters is being able to find any framework in your notes in under 10 seconds. This is the argument for short, tabbed notes over 300-page binders — covered in our comparison of short notes vs long notes.
  • Structure: Every NCA answer must demonstrate legal analysis in the examiner's expected format. An examiner-readable answer structure is as important as legal knowledge.

Bar Exam Preparation Strategy

The provincial bar exam rewards a completely different skill set. Multiple-choice questions do not test your ability to reason — they test whether you know the specific rule. If you do not know that a defendant in Ontario has a specific number of days to file a statement of defence, you cannot guess the answer. Partial knowledge produces wrong answers more often than it produces right ones.

The core preparation skills for bar exams:

  • Rule memorisation: Specific procedural rules with their timelines, exceptions, and province-specific variations.
  • Multiple-choice technique: Process of elimination, question flagging, and time management across 220+ questions (Ontario Barrister exam) in a limited window.
  • Tabbed reference materials: Bar candidates are permitted to bring their materials — knowing exactly where each rule is located, and getting there fast, is a significant advantage.

Do not underestimate the bar exam because it is multiple choice. The volume of rules required, and the precision of recall needed, makes it a serious examination in its own right. Many NCA candidates who passed their NCA subjects on first attempt have struggled with the bar simply because they applied NCA preparation strategies to a very different kind of test.

The Sequence You Cannot Reverse: 2026 Pathway

The mandatory sequence for internationally trained lawyers in 2026 is:

  1. NCA Assessment application — $400 CAD + taxes. Submit transcripts, degree certificates, and certificate of good standing from your home bar association. Processing: approximately 6–8 weeks after all documents are received.
  2. NCA Exams — $500 CAD + taxes per subject. Available twelve times per year. Complete your 5 mandatory subjects plus any additional assigned subjects. Hard-copy open-book, 3 hours each, online proctored.
  3. Legal Research and Writing (LRW) — Mandatory for all candidates assessed after January 1, 2022. Completed through CPLED at cpled.ca. Approximately 8 months part-time. Can overlap with NCA exam preparation.
  4. Indigenous Law and Peoples competency — Mandatory for all candidates as of March 1, 2026. Completed through a CPLED module or approved law school course. Not an exam subject — a standalone competency requirement. Must be complete before the COQ is issued.
  5. Certificate of Qualification (COQ) application — Submitted through the NCA portal after all exams, LRW, and Indigenous Law competency are complete. The COQ is not issued automatically — it is a formal application step. Contact the NCA for current processing times.
  6. Articling — 10–12 months depending on province. Begin applying for articling positions well before you receive your COQ. Competition is significant for internationally trained lawyers.
  7. Provincial bar exams — Written during or after articling, depending on the province. You cannot sit bar exams without the COQ.
  8. Call to the Bar — The end of the road. You are now a lawyer licensed to practise in that province.

No step in this sequence can be reversed or skipped. The NCA COQ is a prerequisite for every provincial law society bar admission programme in the common law provinces and territories of Canada.

The "Bar Exam Is Easier" Trap

Many internationally trained lawyers assume the bar exam must be easier because it is multiple choice. This assumption has ended careers — or at least delayed them by six months.

The NCA and bar exams test different things with different failure modes:

  • On the NCA: If you know the analytical framework, you can construct a competent answer even when uncertain of specific details. The structure carries you. Open-book means you can look up what you cannot recall.
  • On the bar exam: If you do not know the specific rule — the exact filing timeline, the precise exception, the specific form — you cannot reason your way to the right multiple-choice answer. Partial knowledge produces wrong answers more reliably than it produces correct ones. The volume of rules required is genuinely daunting.

Both exams require serious, dedicated preparation. Finishing the NCA is a significant achievement. It is not a warm-up for an easy final hurdle.

Which Should You Focus on First?

You do not have a choice — the NCA is first, always. But within the NCA stage, the order of your subject preparation matters and the strategic choices you make in the first three months set the pace for everything that follows.
