Here is the answer in the first paragraph: For the vast majority of candidates, no. You do not need the textbook.

The NCA exam is open-book. It tests application of law under time pressure, not textbook knowledge. The question is whether you can apply the law in 45 minutes, not whether you have read the source text.

This article explains why textbooks create the illusion of thoroughness — and why that illusion costs you marks.

---

## The Open-Book Trap

Candidates hear "open-book" and think: *I need comprehensive materials. I need the textbook. I need to be thorough.*

This is catastrophically wrong.

Open-book means the exam tests **application speed**, not recall. You have 45 minutes per question. You cannot read and apply simultaneously. You cannot search through 600 pages and write a coherent answer in the time available.

**What open-book actually requires:**
- Materials you know cold
- Tabbed and organised notes
- Quick reference capability
- Frameworks at your fingertips

**What open-book does not require:**
- Comprehensive textbooks
- Case summaries
- Background material
- Everything you might possibly need

## The Textbook Illusion

Textbooks create a feeling of safety. They are thick, comprehensive, authoritative. Purchasing them feels like responsible preparation.

In practice, candidates who buy textbooks:
- Read the first two chapters thoroughly
- Skim the rest
- Never reference them during the exam
- Fail because they cannot find what they need quickly

The pattern is consistent across the WhatsApp groups I monitored:

> "I purchased all three books for each exam only to realise I had minimal use of them."

This was not one candidate. This was a pattern.

## When Textbooks Might Help

There are limited circumstances where textbooks add value:

1. **You have no legal background whatsoever**
   - First exposure to common law
   - Need foundational understanding
   - Even then, condensed introductions are better

2. **You are preparing over 6+ months**
   - Time to read comprehensively
   - Time to distil your own notes
   - Most candidates do not have this luxury

3. **You learn best from extensive reading**
   - Some candidates genuinely prefer depth
   - But they still need condensed notes for the exam

For 90% of candidates, textbooks are unnecessary expense and unnecessary weight.

## FAQ

**Q: But the NCA recommends textbooks.**

The NCA lists recommended readings. They do not mandate them. The exam tests application of principles, not recall of textbook content.

**Q: What if I need more detail on a topic?**

Our notes include references to key cases and statutes. If you genuinely need more depth (rare), free online resources (CanLII, SCC decisions) provide it.

**Q: Should I buy used textbooks to save money?**

If you are determined to have textbooks, used is fine. But consider whether you will use them at all before spending even $50.

**Q: I already bought textbooks. Should I still buy notes?**

Yes. Use textbooks for background if you have time. Use notes for exam preparation. The notes are designed for the exam hall.

**Q: What about for Constitutional Law? The textbooks seem essential.**

Constitutional Law has significant content, but the frameworks are still limited. Our notes cover division of powers, Charter, Oakes test, and Aboriginal rights — everything the exam tests. Textbooks add background, not exam value.
