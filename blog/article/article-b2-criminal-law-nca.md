## Actus Reus and Mens Rea — The Foundation

Every criminal offence requires proof of actus reus (the guilty act) and mens rea (the guilty mind). The analysis follows a clear structure.

### Actus Reus Analysis

1. **Identify the prohibited conduct**
   - What act or omission is prohibited?
   - Is it defined in the Criminal Code or common law?

2. **Establish causation (if applicable)**
   - Factual causation: "but for" the act, would the result have occurred?
   - Legal causation: was the act a substantial and operating cause?

3. **Check for absence of justification**
   - Even if actus reus is established, justification may negate liability

### Mens Rea Analysis

1. **Identify the required mental state**
   - Intention (purposeful)
   - Knowledge (awareness)
   - Recklessness (conscious disregard of risk)
   - Negligence (objective standard)

2. **Determine subjective vs objective**
   - Subjective: what did the accused actually think/feel?
   - Objective: what would a reasonable person have thought/felt?

3. **Apply to facts**
   - Did the accused possess the required mental state?

## Criminal Defences — The Complete Framework

Defences are a major component of the Criminal Law exam. You must know when each applies and how to analyse them.

### 1. Mistake of Fact (Section 19 and Common Law)

**Section 19:** Ignorance of the law is no excuse.

**Mistake of fact:** Can negate mens rea if the mistake was honest and reasonable.

**Analysis:**
- Was the mistake one of fact (not law)?
- Was the mistake honest?
- Was the mistake reasonable?
- If yes, mens rea may be negated

### 2. Intoxication

**General rule:** Intoxication is not a defence to general intent offences.

**Daviault exception:** Extreme intoxication may negate voluntariness for specific intent offences.

**Robinson distinction:** Intoxication is not a defence to crimes of violence.

**Analysis:**
- Is the offence general intent or specific intent?
- Was the intoxication extreme (akin to automatism)?
- Does the offence involve violence?

### 3. Self-Defence (Section 34 CC)

The post-2012 s. 34 provides a single framework for all self-defence claims.

**Three-part test:**

1. **Reasonable grounds:** Did the accused reasonably believe force was being used against them?

2. **Defensive purpose:** Did the accused act for the purpose of defending themselves?

3. **Reasonable response:** Was the response reasonable in the circumstances?

**Factors to consider:** Nature of force, immediacy, role of accused, size/age/gender, history, alternatives.

### 4. Necessity

**Requirements:**
1. Urgent situation of imminent peril
2. No reasonable legal alternative
3. Proportionality between harm inflicted and harm avoided

**Limitation:** Cannot be used for murder or serious violence.

### 5. Duress

**Requirements:**
1. Threat of death or serious bodily harm
2. Reasonable belief threat will be carried out
3. No safe avenue of escape
4. Proportionality between harm threatened and harm inflicted

**Limitation:** Cannot be used for murder or serious violence.

## Charter Rights in Criminal Proceedings

The Charter provides important protections that frequently appear on exams.

### Section 8 — Search and Seizure

**Protection:** Against unreasonable search and seizure.

**Analysis:**
1. Was there a search or seizure?
2. Was it reasonable?
   - Was there lawful authority (warrant, consent, exigent circumstances)?
   - Was the search conducted reasonably?

### Section 9 — Arbitrary Detention

**Protection:** Against arbitrary detention or imprisonment.

**Analysis:**
1. Was there detention?
2. Was it arbitrary (no lawful basis)?

### Section 10 — Rights Upon Arrest

**Protections:**
- Right to be informed of reasons (s. 10(a))
- Right to counsel (s. 10(b))

**Section 10(b) analysis:**
1. Was there a detention or arrest?
2. Were rights to counsel provided?
3. Was the accused given a reasonable opportunity to exercise the right?

### Section 11(d) — Presumption of Innocence

**Protection:** Right to be presumed innocent until proven guilty.

**Analysis:**
1. Does the provision create a presumption?
2. Does the presumption relate to an essential element of the offence?
3. Is the presumption irrebuttable or reverse onus?

If yes, the provision may violate s. 11(d).

## Practice Question with Model Answer

**Question:**

> D, heavily intoxicated after drinking at a bar, gets into an argument with V. D punches V, causing serious injury. D is charged with aggravated assault. D claims he was too drunk to know what he was doing and acted in self-defence because V threatened him first.
>
> Advise D on his potential liability and available defences.

**Model Answer:**

**1. Issue Identification**

D faces a charge of aggravated assault (s. 268 CC). The issues are: (a) whether actus reus and mens rea are established; (b) whether intoxication provides a defence; (c) whether self-defence applies.

**2. Actus Reus Analysis**

Aggravated assault requires: (a) assault (application of force without consent); (b) wounding, maiming, disfiguring, or endangering life.

D punched V, causing serious injury. The actus reus is established. The punch was a voluntary act causing injury.

**3. Mens Rea Analysis**

Assault requires intentional application of force or recklessness as to whether force is applied. D intentionally punched V. Mens rea is established.

Intoxication does not negate the mens rea for general intent offences like assault. D's intoxication is irrelevant to mens rea.

**4. Defences**

*Intoxication:*
Aggravated assault is a general intent offence. Intoxication is not a defence. The Daviault exception (extreme intoxication akin to automatism) does not apply to crimes of violence under Robinson. D's defence fails.

*Self-Defence (s. 34):*

1. **Reasonable grounds:** Did D reasonably believe force was being used against him? The facts indicate V "threatened" D first. This may establish reasonable grounds.

2. **Defensive purpose:** Did D act for defensive purposes? The facts suggest D was responding to a threat. This element may be satisfied.

3. **Reasonable response:** Was punching V reasonable? Factors: D was heavily intoxicated (reduced capacity to assess), but the response (serious injury) may be disproportionate to a verbal threat. This element is questionable.

**Conclusion on defences:** Intoxication fails. Self-defence is arguable but weak on proportionality.

**5. Conclusion**

D is likely liable for aggravated assault. The actus reus and mens rea are established. Intoxication is not a defence. Self-defence is weak on the proportionality element. D should consider plea negotiations.
