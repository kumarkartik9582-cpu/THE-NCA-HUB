Criminal Law is often underestimated by NCA candidates who assume their prior legal training has prepared them sufficiently. While foreign criminal law experience helps, the Canadian Criminal Code has specific nuances, and the NCA exam tests particular analytical approaches.

This guide focuses on what actually appears on the exam — not the entire Criminal Code, but the high-frequency topics that determine pass or fail.

## What the Criminal Law Exam Actually Tests

The NCA Criminal Law exam focuses on core criminal law principles rather than procedural or evidentiary rules (which are covered in Evidence and Criminal Procedure, separate subjects).

**High-frequency topics:**

| Topic | Frequency | Priority |
|-------|-----------|----------|
| Actus reus (including causation) | 95% | Essential |
| Mens rea (intention, recklessness, negligence) | 95% | Essential |
| Major defences (self-defence, necessity, duress, intoxication) | 85% | Essential |
| Inchoate offences (attempt, conspiracy, counselling) | 60% | High |
| Parties to offences | 50% | Medium |
| Regulatory offences (strict liability) | 40% | Medium |

## Actus Reus — The Physical Element

Every Criminal Law answer starts with actus reus analysis.

### Components of Actus Reus

1. **Voluntary act** (or omission where duty exists)
2. **Causation** (for result crimes)
3. **Circumstances** (where specified in the offence definition)

### Causation Analysis

For crimes like murder or assault causing bodily harm, causation is often the central issue.

**Factual causation:** The "but for" test — would the result have occurred without the accused's actions?

**Legal causation:** Was the result a foreseeable consequence of the accused's actions? Was there an intervening act that breaks the chain of causation?

## Mens Rea — The Mental Element

The mental element distinguishes criminal from civil liability. The NCA exam tests various levels of fault.

### Subjective vs Objective Standards

**Subjective fault:** What did the accused actually intend or foresee?
- Intention (purpose or knowledge)
- Recklessness (foresight of risk)
- Wilful blindness (deliberate ignorance)

**Objective fault:** What would a reasonable person have foreseen?
- Criminal negligence
- Some regulatory offences

### Specific Intent vs General Intent

**Specific intent:** Intention to achieve a particular result beyond the immediate act (e.g., breaking and entering *with intent* to commit an indictable offence).

**General intent:** Simply intending to do the prohibited act (e.g., common assault).

**Important:** Intoxication may negate specific intent but generally not general intent.

## Major Defences

The defences most commonly tested are:

### Self-Defence (s. 34)

The modern self-defence provision requires:
1. **Reasonable grounds** to believe force is being used against the person
2. **Defensive purpose** — acting to protect oneself
3. **Reasonable response** — the force used must be proportionate

The "reasonable person" standard incorporates the circumstances, including the accused's personal characteristics relevant to whether the response was reasonable.

### Necessity and Duress

**Necessity:** Breaking the law to avoid greater harm (imminent peril, no reasonable alternative, proportionate harm).

**Duress:** Committing an offence due to threats of death or bodily harm (imminent threat, no safe avenue of escape, proportionate response).

**Key distinction:** Necessity typically involves natural threats; duress involves threats from other persons.

### Intoxication

The law on intoxication changed significantly with *R v Brown*, 2022 SCC 18.

**General rule:** Intoxication is not a defence to general intent crimes.

**Exception:** Extreme intoxication akin to automatism may negate voluntariness for any crime, but the threshold is extremely high.

**Specific intent crimes:** Intoxication may negate the specific intent element.

## The Criminal Law Answer Structure

Unlike Administrative or Constitutional Law, Criminal Law answers follow a sequential analysis that mirrors the elements of offences.

### Template for Offence Analysis

**1. IDENTIFY THE OFFENCE**
- State the offence charged
- Identify the applicable Criminal Code section

**2. ACTUS REUS**
- Define the physical elements required
- Apply to facts — did the accused's conduct meet the definition?
- Address any causation issues if relevant

**3. MENS REA**
- Identify the required mental element (intention, recklessness, negligence)
- Apply to facts — did the accused have the required state of mind?
- Distinguish between subjective and objective standards

**4. DEFENCES** (if raised by facts)
- Identify potential defences
- Apply elements of each defence to facts
- Conclude on availability

**5. CONCLUSION**
- Summarise liability for each offence/defence
- Provide final advice to client

## Common Mistakes on Criminal Law Exams

### 1. Skipping the Basics
Candidates often jump to complex defences without establishing basic actus reus and mens rea. Always start with the elements.

### 2. Confusing Defences
Self-defence, necessity, and duress have distinct elements. Do not conflate them.

### 3. Ignoring Causation
For result crimes, causation is often the dispositive issue. Do not assume it is established just because the accused hit the victim.

### 4. Outdated Law
Ensure you know the current law on intoxication post-*Brown* (2022).
