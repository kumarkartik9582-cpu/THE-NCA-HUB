## Standard of Review — The Vavilov Framework

This is the core of Administrative Law. Every exam includes at least one Vavilov question. Many include two.

### The Starting Point: The Presumption of Reasonableness

Since *Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration) v Vavilov*, 2019 SCC 65, the default standard of review is **reasonableness**. This presumption applies to all administrative decisions unless an exception applies.

**The analytical sequence:**

1. **Identify the decision-maker** — Is it an administrative tribunal, minister, or other delegated decision-maker?

2. **Apply the Vavilov presumption** — Reasonableness is the default.

3. **Check for correctness exceptions** — Does the question fall into one of the categories where correctness applies?

4. **Apply the appropriate standard** — Reasonableness or correctness.

### When Correctness Applies — The Exceptions

The Vavilov framework identifies categories where correctness review applies:

**Category 1: Questions of Constitutional Law**
- Division of powers (federalism)
- Charter questions
- Other constitutional issues

**Category 2: Questions of Jurisdictional Importance**
- Questions that have "general importance to the legal system as a whole"
- Questions that are "central to the legal system"

**Category 3: Questions Where the Legislature Has Indicated Correctness**
- Statutory appeal mechanisms
- Privative clauses (which trigger correctness analysis)

### What Reasonableness Review Means in Practice

Reasonableness is not perfection. It is a **defensible outcome** based on the legal and factual constraints before the decision-maker.

The Vavilov framework identifies two ways to assess reasonableness:

**1. Internal Logic of the Decision**
- Does the decision make sense on its own terms?
- Are the reasoning steps coherent?
- Does the conclusion follow from the premises?

**2. Justification in Relation to Facts and Law**
- Does the decision engage with the relevant legal constraints?
- Does it address the factual record?
- Are the findings supported by evidence?

### Worked Example: Vavilov Analysis

**Fact Pattern:**

> The Immigration and Refugee Board denied A's refugee claim. A seeks judicial review. The Board's decision contains minimal reasoning and does not address A's key evidence of country conditions.

**Analysis:**

1. **Decision-maker:** Immigration and Refugee Board — administrative tribunal.

2. **Presumption:** Reasonableness applies.

3. **Exceptions:** No constitutional question, no general importance, no statutory appeal mechanism. Reasonableness confirmed.

4. **Reasonableness assessment:**
   - **Internal logic:** The decision lacks coherent reasoning. The conclusion does not follow from stated premises.
   - **Justification:** The Board failed to engage with key evidence. This is a failure of justification.

5. **Conclusion:** The decision is unreasonable and should be set aside.

## Procedural Fairness — The Baker Framework

Procedural fairness is the second essential topic. It appears on almost every exam.

### When Is the Duty Triggered?

The duty of procedural fairness arises when:

1. A statutory power is exercised
2. The decision affects rights, privileges, or interests
3. The decision is not purely legislative or policy-based

### The Five Baker Factors

*Baker v Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration)*, [1999] 2 SCR 817 established five factors for determining the **level** of procedural fairness owed:

1. **Nature of the decision** — Is it administrative, quasi-judicial, or legislative?
2. **Nature of the statutory scheme** — Is there a right of appeal? Is the decision final?
3. **Importance of the decision to the individual** — How significant are the consequences?
4. **Legitimate expectations** — Did the decision-maker create an expectation of certain procedures?
5. **Respect for procedural choices of the decision-maker** — What procedures does the tribunal normally follow?

### The Content of Procedural Fairness

Once the level is determined, the content may include:

- **Notice** — Of the case to be met
- **Disclosure** — Of relevant information
- **Opportunity to be heard** — To respond to concerns
- **Impartial decision-maker** — No reasonable apprehension of bias
- **Reasons** — Explanation for the decision

### Worked Example: Procedural Fairness Analysis

**Fact Pattern:**

> A university student faces disciplinary proceedings for academic misconduct. The committee meets in private, does not provide A with the evidence against him, and issues a decision without reasons.

**Analysis:**

1. **Duty triggered:** Yes — disciplinary proceeding affecting academic standing.

2. **Baker factors assessment:**
   - Nature: Quasi-judicial disciplinary proceeding — higher fairness
   - Statutory scheme: University regulations, limited appeal — higher fairness
   - Importance: Academic career at stake — higher fairness
   - Legitimate expectations: University policy promises certain procedures — higher fairness
   - Respect for choices: Committee normally provides some process — moderate

3. **Level of fairness:** High — approaching natural justice.

4. **Content required:** Notice, disclosure, opportunity to respond, impartial tribunal, reasons.

5. **Breach:** Failure to disclose evidence, private hearing, no reasons — multiple breaches.

6. **Remedy:** Judicial review; likely quashing of decision.

## Judicial Review — Availability and Procedure

The third essential topic is when judicial review is available and what remedies it offers.

### Statutory Right of Appeal vs Judicial Review

**First question:** Is there a statutory right of appeal?

- If **yes** — The appeal route must generally be exhausted first.
- If **no** — Judicial review may be available.

### Privative Clauses

A privative clause attempts to restrict or exclude judicial review. Under Vavilov, privative clauses trigger **correctness** review of the clause's interpretation — not deference to the administrative decision-maker.

### Remedies in Judicial Review

| Remedy | When Used | Effect |
|--------|-----------|--------|
| **Certiorari** | To quash an unlawful decision | Decision is voided |
| **Prohibition** | To prevent an unlawful act | Act is prevented |
| **Mandamus** | To compel performance of a duty | Duty must be performed |
| **Declaration** | To clarify legal rights | Legal position stated |
| **Injunction** | To restrain unlawful conduct | Conduct restrained |

## The Administrative Law Answer Template

This is the structure for a standard Administrative Law question. Memorise it. Use it. Every time.

### Template Structure

```
1. ISSUE IDENTIFICATION
   - Identify the legal issue(s) raised
   - Note the decision-maker and type of decision

2. STANDARD OF REVIEW
   - Apply Vavilov presumption (reasonableness)
   - Check for correctness exceptions
   - State the applicable standard

3. APPLICABLE STANDARD ANALYSIS
   
   If REASONABLENESS:
   - Internal logic assessment
   - Justification assessment
   - Conclusion on reasonableness
   
   If CORRECTNESS:
   - State the correct legal test
   - Apply to facts
   - Conclusion on correctness

4. PROCEDURAL FAIRNESS (if raised)
   - Duty triggered? (Baker threshold)
   - Level of fairness (Baker factors)
   - Content required
   - Breach analysis

5. REMEDIES
   - Appropriate remedy for each issue
   - Justification for remedy choice

6. CONCLUSION
   - Summary of findings
   - Final advice to client
```

## Practice Question with Model Answer

**Question:**

> The Environmental Review Board denied B's application to develop a waterfront property. The Board's decision was based on environmental impact concerns but contained minimal reasoning and did not address B's evidence that similar developments had been approved nearby. B seeks your advice on judicial review.
>
> Advise B on: (a) the applicable standard of review; (b) whether the decision is vulnerable to challenge; and (c) available remedies.

**Model Answer:**

**1. Issue Identification**

This case involves a challenge to an administrative decision (Environmental Review Board denial) on grounds of inadequate reasoning and failure to address relevant evidence. The issues are: (a) standard of review; (b) reasonableness of the decision; and (c) appropriate remedies.

**2. Standard of Review**

The default standard under *Vavilov* is reasonableness. This presumption applies to the Environmental Review Board's decision.

Checking for correctness exceptions:
- No constitutional question is raised
- No question of general importance to the legal system
- No statutory appeal mechanism indicated

**The applicable standard is reasonableness.**

**3. Reasonableness Analysis**

*Internal Logic:*
The Board's decision lacks coherent reasoning. The conclusion (denial based on environmental concerns) is not supported by articulated reasoning steps. The minimal reasoning provided does not explain why B's specific application failed.

*Justification:*
The Board failed to engage with B's evidence regarding similar approved developments. Under Vavilov, reasonableness requires decision-makers to address relevant evidence and explain why contrary conclusions were reached. The failure to address this evidence undermines the decision's justification.

*Conclusion:* The decision appears unreasonable on both internal logic and justification grounds.

**4. Procedural Fairness**

While B has not explicitly raised procedural fairness, the inadequate reasoning may engage the duty of fairness. Under *Baker*, the level of fairness owed depends on the nature of the decision (significant property interest), importance to the individual (major development), and statutory context. The failure to provide adequate reasons may itself constitute a breach.

**5. Remedies**

If the decision is found unreasonable, appropriate remedies include:
- **Certiorari** to quash the unreasonable decision
- **Mandamus** to compel the Board to reconsider with adequate reasoning

**6. Conclusion**

B has strong grounds for judicial review. The decision is vulnerable on reasonableness grounds due to inadequate reasoning and failure to address relevant evidence. B should apply for judicial review seeking certiorari and mandamus.
